What the app is, and what it isn't

The PHC Planner is an offline treatment-plan framework. Tell it what is wrong with each plant and it tells you what can be done about it, by which method, how many times a year and in which months — then packs that into the fewest visits that still respect every window. It does not talk to SingleOps, and it carries no rates and no prices. Rates come off the label; money comes from the catalog. A plan is not a quote.

Before the first visit

Arborist

Do this in the office on wifi. The first load is the only one that needs a connection — after it, the app, the whole pest knowledge base and the PDF and ZIP libraries live on the device.

Install it

  1. Open the PHC Planner link in Safari. Only Safari can install to the iOS home screen.
  2. Share → Add to Home Screen → Add. It appears as “PHC Planner”.
  3. Open it from the home screen once, still on wifi, and let it finish loading. After that it runs with no signal at all.
  4. In Files → Browse → ⋯ → Edit, switch OneDrive on. You need it at the end of every visit.

Know the four tabs

  • Plans — every landscape you have ever built stays on the device. Search by client, lead #, address or plant.
  • Landscape — the plan you are working on. Where the visit happens.
  • Library and Reference — the pest and treatment knowledge base, to look things up on site. Not covered here.
  • ↻ Update installs a new version; 🐞 files a bug with the version, screen and device already filled in. Update on wifi, not on a property.
One-off, and it is not part of the plan At the bottom of the treatment plan screen sits Edit available products. That list decides which chemical treatments the app will even offer, and it is a device setting — switching something off hides it on every plan you build afterwards. Set it once against what RTEC actually stocks. If a treatment you expected is missing, look here before assuming the app is wrong.

The walkthrough

Arborist

One landscape in Bethesda: two hemlocks, an eighteen-plant boxwood hedge, a specimen white oak, a shade bed of azaleas and an ornamental cherry. Recordings are click-to-play.

Step 1 · The job
STEP 1

The job

Five fields at the top of the Landscape tab, and two of them decide things a long way downstream.

  • SingleOps lead # first, before any photo. It becomes the front of every photo filename — 51842-1, 51842-3 — and the front of the export folder name. Leave it blank and the office renames five files by hand.
  • Client name in house format: Last, First. That is the string Conor matches against the SingleOps client record.
  • Work address sets the jurisdiction (VA / MD / DC), and the jurisdiction drives every compliance flag in the plan. Wrong address, wrong flags. You can override it.
  • Inspection date lands in the report header and in both the folder and file names.
Step 2 · A plant group
STEP 2

One entry per plant group, not per stem

Eighteen boxwoods in one hedge are a single entry with a quantity of 18. Two hemlocks flanking a property line are one entry with a quantity of 2. The group number follows through to the map pin, the photo filename and the inventory row.

  • The label is where it is, not what it is. Pair flanking the north property line — a technician who has never seen the property reads this first.
  • Quantity and DBH are what the office prices against. A hedge run without a count cannot be quoted.
  • Crown loss % only appears once you tick a problem that has a treatment threshold attached. When it appears it is doing real work — some treatments are withdrawn above a certain amount of dieback. Estimate it honestly.
  • Arborist notes go to the crew, not the client. What you saw, and what has to happen before anything is applied.
Step 3 · Problems
STEP 3

Tick what you actually see

Below the details is the pest list filtered to that host and grouped by category — adelgids, mites, armored scales, blights. Ticking a problem is what puts treatments into the plan. Nothing else does.

  • A plant with no problem ticked gets no treatment and no line in the proposal, however much you wrote in the notes.
  • Problems not listed has its own free-text box at the top. It is recorded and it prints, but it carries no treatment — the report says so explicitly.
  • Broad host range & abiotic at the bottom holds the 36 problems that are not host-specific. Worth opening for anything that looks like a site problem rather than a pest.
Step 4 · Fine-tune on map
STEP 4

Put the pin on the plant

Re-record GPS averages readings and reports what it managed — ±6 m · 12 readings. Under a canopy that is often not good enough, so drag the imagery until the crosshair sits on the plant.

  • The dashed ring is the phone's guess, drawn at its own stated accuracy. The other numbered pins are the plant groups you have already recorded, labelled by species, so you can see the shape of the property as you go.
  • Saved by hand reads placed on map instead of an accuracy figure — and that is the more trustworthy of the two. Back to GPS undoes it.
  • This matters more on PHC than on tree work. A program sends a technician back several times a year, often someone who has never been. Fine-tune anything a stranger could not find from the label alone.
  • Needs a connection for the imagery. No signal: record the GPS fix anyway and fine-tune from the truck.
Step 5 · Soil care
STEP 5

Soil care — the last step

Property-wide programs, not tied to any pest. Each has an Apply to scope instead of a plant: ornamentals, mature trees, both, or specific plants you pick off the treatment plan.

  • Standard Soil Care — 2 drenches, Mar–Apr and Sep–Oct. Resilience Support — 1 drench, Jul–Aug.
  • Prescriptive Soil Correction only when a lab report exists or is on its way — its whole point is that products and rates come from the analysis. On a first visit, sell the sampling and prescribe at the next one.
  • Soil sample analysis takes a count and a description of where the samples come from. Both print, and both end up in the proposal line.
  • Arborist notes (program stage) is your note to the office: what to lead with, what the client actually asked about.
Step 6 · Build treatment plan
STEP 6

Build the plan

The app takes every problem you ticked, works out which treatments apply, how many applications a year each needs and which months they can happen in, then packs them into the fewest visits that respect every window.

  • For this landscape: 5 visits/yr · 14 applications · 1 one-time. That one-time is the soil sampling, scheduled ASAP before the program starts.
  • The defaults are a starting point, not a decision. Everything the app pre-selected is a tickbox you can change, and the count of applications within the reference range is a dropdown.
  • Tap any plant to review it before you show the client anything.
Step 7 · Flags
STEP 7

Read the flags on site

Each problem lists its chemical options ranked, with Preferred first and the rest folded under Alternatives; non-chemical options sit under Cultural. Open any option for its product, repeat interval and compliance flags.

  • Maryland restricts neonicotinoids — imidacloprid, dinotefuran, clothianidin, thiamethoxam. Certified applicator only, and product status has to be verified. Six of the treatments in this plan carry it.
  • Pollinator hazard — do not apply to bee-attractive plants before or during bloom. A live constraint on the azalea and the cherry here.
  • Read them while you can still change the selection. They print on the report, but by then the client has seen it.
  • The flags follow the jurisdiction. The same landscape in Virginia would not show the neonicotinoid restriction at all.
Step 8 · Visits
STEP 8

Visits, and the annual calendar

This is the part the office cannot reconstruct from a list of treatments, and the reason the export is worth more than a text message.

  • Each visit names its month, how many applications it carries and how flexible its dates are — Visit 2 · Late Apr · 4 applications · flexible Early Apr – Late Apr.
  • ⇆ also fits Visit 5, 6 means that application's window spans more than one visit. Those are the ones the office can move when a route needs rebalancing — and the only ones.
  • The annual calendar shows the same work by month instead of by visit — eight active months here, March to October. Use it when a client asks when you are actually on the property.
Step 9 · Export
STEP 9

Export the bundle

Take the folder (.zip) — it is the PDF plus everything else — and then take the PDF on its own as well, so the office can read it on a phone without unzipping.

  • The site map is fetched when you export, not when you record the pins. With a connection you get aerial imagery; without one, a drawn fallback. If you exported with no bars, export again from the truck and upload that one.
  • Share / copy plan text is a plain-text summary for a quick message. It is not a substitute for the export and the office cannot build from it.

Getting it off the phone

Arborist

Do this before you leave the property, or at the latest before the end of the day. A plan that only exists on your phone does not exist.

iPhone & iPad

  1. Tap Export folder (.zip), then Export PDF report. Safari saves both to Downloads.
  2. Tap the downloads arrow in the Safari address bar, then the file name. It opens in Files.
  3. Touch and hold the ZIP → Share → Save to Files → OneDrive, and pick the folder below. Repeat for the loose PDF.
  4. Wait for the OneDrive tick. On a weak signal it queues — leave the app open until it clears.

If there was no signal on site

  1. Everything works offline except the satellite site map, which is fetched at the moment you export.
  2. Export anyway. The plan is saved on the device regardless, and stays under Plans.
  3. From the truck, open the plan again and export a second time, so the office gets aerial imagery instead of the drawn fallback.
  4. Upload that second bundle, and delete the first from Downloads so nobody files the wrong one.

Back at the office: building the program

Conor

Open the report beside SingleOps. Pages 1–3 give you the inventory and what gets treated; page 4 gives you the visits. Work in that order.

Job settings, before any line

  • Operation PHC (or IPC) · Job Type Recurring · Per Visit invoicing · Custom frequency on the tier's recurrence preset
  • Specify Items for each Visit switched on
  • Season start and end — this plan runs Mar to Oct
  • Eligible for Renewal ticked · Terms Due on receipt · Tax Location set
  • Sales Rep = the arborist · Job Name = the visit location or a short description, with no year in it

Tier, tags and payment

  • Tier is a judgement call, not an app output. Five visits with systemics and soil care sits at CPP Plus or Premier — confirm with the arborist, then tag 1_CPP_Plus or 1_CPP_Premier.
  • A targeted (non-CPP) program must carry PHC Targeted Program. There is no tag by elimination.
  • Other job tags: property size S-, site conditions P-, any IPM- targets, and the Lead Source carried from the lead.
  • Payment: AutoPay (card on file), invoice on completion, or prepay with the 5% discount if paid by 28 Feb. Mosquito programs are prepay only; this one is not a mosquito program.
Set the job type at creation Changing a job to Recurring after the fact deletes payments. There is no quiet recovery from that — get it right when you create the job.

Visits and treatment tags

Name each visit with its treatment code and a number — SIN #1, Fall Bio-Stim — and give it exactly one PHC_ treatment tag matching the name. That tag is what the Route Planner filters on and what treatment counts are built from.

Where the app and SingleOps disagree The planner builds visits to minimise trips, so Visit 2 (Late Apr) carries a systemic drench, a foliar fungicide and a bio-stim round in one stop. SingleOps wants one treatment tag per visit. For any mixed visit you either split it into separately tagged visits on the same date, or name it for its dominant round and let the rest ride along. Split it when the rounds need different technicians or different equipment; otherwise name it for the dominant round. The ⇆ also fits markers on page 4 tell you which applications can move without breaking their window.

Notes, and one compliance step

  • Client Notes — two or three sentences on what the program does and why.
  • Crew Notes — access, gates, pets, and anything that has to happen before an application. Here: two boxwoods at the downspout to cut out before any foliar work.
  • Internal Notes — the arborist's program-stage note. Here: lead with the hemlocks; the oak CRZ is the priority for soil correction if the lab supports it.
  • Under 1,000 characters across all three.
Maryland, before anything is applied This property is in Maryland, so the sensitive-individual registry check has to happen before any application, and the neonicotinoid work is certified-applicator only. The report flags the restriction; it does not do the registry check for you. Never copy registry data into SingleOps.

Before you hand it over

  • Recurring, Per Visit, Eligible for Renewal, no year in the job name
  • Operation, Sales Rep, Terms and Tax Location set
  • One group per round, each with all four lines; labour in MHrs
  • Every visit has exactly one PHC_ tag, matching its name
  • Tier tag present, or PHC Targeted Program
  • Payment arrangement in place before anything is scheduled
  • Report PDF and the photos attached to the job
  • Add-ons on the proposal as Recommended or Optional lines, never in the notes

What this doesn't do

  • No rates, no prices, no quote. The planner names the active ingredient, the method, the frequency and the window. How much product, and how much money, come from the label and the SingleOps catalog. If a client asks for a number off the report, there isn't one.
  • No SingleOps connection. Nothing in the ZIP is an import file. The report is a source document a person reads while typing — which is why it is laid out in the order the program is built.
  • No diagnosis. It works from the problems you tick. Tick the wrong pest and it will confidently plan the wrong year of treatments; the oak's bacterial leaf scorch even carries a flag telling you to confirm by lab where symptoms are ambiguous.
  • No compliance clearance. It flags the Maryland neonicotinoid restriction and pollinator hazard, but the sensitive-individual registry check, applicator certification and current product status are still yours.
  • No tier, no pricing, no renewal logic. CPP tier, add-ons, prepay and renewal eligibility are decided in SingleOps by the arborist and the office.
  • Nothing is synced. Plans live on one device until the ZIP is in OneDrive. A lost iPad is a lost plan.
